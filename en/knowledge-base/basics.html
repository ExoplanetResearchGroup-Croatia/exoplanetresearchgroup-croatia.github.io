Exoplanet basics

Draft, under review. This text was written from general knowledge and has not yet been reviewed by the group.

What is an exoplanet

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. The first ones were found in 1992 around the pulsar PSR B1257+12, and the first planet around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, in 1995. Several thousand have been confirmed since; the NASA Exoplanet Archive (opens in a new tab) keeps the current count.

How they are found

Most exoplanets cannot be seen directly because the light of their star outshines them. They are found through the traces they leave on the star:

  • Transit method: the planet passes in front of the star and its brightness drops briefly. This method has found the most planets, mainly with the Kepler and TESS space telescopes.
  • Radial velocity: the planet and the star orbit a common centre of mass, so the star wobbles slightly towards and away from us. This shows up as a shift of the lines in its spectrum.
  • Direct imaging: large, young planets far from their star can be photographed when the starlight is blocked.
  • Gravitational microlensing: a star with a planet briefly magnifies the light of a more distant star behind it, and the planet adds a short extra signal.

The transit

A transit is only visible when we see the planet's orbit almost edge-on. While the planet crosses the disc of the star, it blocks part of its light. The fraction of light blocked, the transit depth, is roughly the ratio of the area of the planet's disc to the area of the star's disc. A Jupiter-sized planet in front of a Sun-like star dims it by about 1 %, an Earth-sized planet by only about 0.01 %.

A transit usually lasts from one to several hours and repeats every time the planet completes an orbit.

What a transit tells us

  • Size of the planet: from the transit depth and the known size of the star.
  • Orbital period: from the time between successive transits.
  • Inclination and distance from the star: from the shape and duration of the transit.
  • Mass and density: when transits are combined with radial-velocity measurements.
  • Other planets in the system: when transits come late or early compared with the prediction (transit timing variations, TTV).
  • Atmosphere: large telescopes measure how the transit depth changes with wavelength.

Why amateur observations matter

Predicted transit times become less precise over time, because small errors in the orbital period add up from orbit to orbit. New measurements, including those from small telescopes, keep the predictions accurate. Projects such as the Exoplanet Transit Database and ExoClock work on exactly this. Space missions such as Ariel also need accurate transit times so that their observing time is well used.
