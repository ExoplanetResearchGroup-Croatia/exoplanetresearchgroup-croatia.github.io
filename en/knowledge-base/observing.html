Observing guide

Draft, under review. This text was written from general knowledge and has not yet been reviewed by the group.

Before the night: choosing a target

  • The transit must be deep enough for your equipment. With a small telescope, start with transits at least 1 % deep (about 10 millimagnitudes).
  • The whole transit, plus at least 30 to 60 minutes before it starts and after it ends, must fall in the night while the target is high enough above the horizon, roughly above 30°.
  • The star must be bright enough for a good signal-to-noise ratio, but not so bright that it saturates the camera.
  • The field of view needs comparison stars of similar brightness and colour.
  • Transit predictions are available from the Exoplanet Transit Database and ExoClock.

Equipment

For transits of Jupiter-sized planets, a small telescope with a camera and a tracking mount is enough. Guiding helps keep the stars on the same pixels all night, and a filter, for example R or V, reduces the differences in atmospheric dimming between stars of different colours.

PH-13: equipment the group uses or recommends (telescope, camera, filters, mount)

Set-up and recording

  1. Synchronise the computer clock with an accurate time source, for example over NTP. The time of every frame must be correct to the second.
  2. Place the target and comparison stars in the field of view and keep them in the same place all night.
  3. Choose an exposure at which neither the target nor the comparison stars saturate. Many observers defocus slightly to spread the light over more pixels.
  4. Record without interruption from at least 30 to 60 minutes before the transit starts until the same time after it ends. Without this baseline it is hard to measure the depth and timing of the transit.
  5. Take calibration frames: bias, darks and flats.

After the night

Note the observing conditions: clouds, Moon, wind and interruptions. Then continue with data reduction.
